\section*{Summary}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{New measurement.} With the director--flow coupling switched off (shear susceptibility $\chi_\mathrm{HI}=0$), the 2D MPCD nematic is isotropic:
$K_1=K_3=43.8\,\kT$, $K_1/K_3=1.00$, as Shendruk \& Yeomans found in 3D with the same switch. With the coupling on, the fluctuation constants follow the
flow-alignment parameter $\lambda$: $K_1/K_3=0.49$, 1.05, 1.49, 1.97 at $\lambda=-2$, 0, 1, 2. The flow softens the mode it rotates most; a mode with zero
coupling keeps the bare value. The rotational viscosity is unchanged, $\gamma_1\approx60$.
\item \textbf{The open question.} Do the defects respond to the bare isotropic stiffness, or to the effective anisotropic one? Two limiting readings
are used throughout:
\textbf{A} (bare): the anisotropy lives only in the noise (an anisotropic effective temperature); mean structures feel $K_1=K_3\approx44$.
\textbf{B} (effective): the flow coupling also renormalises the response; mean structures feel $K_1\approx43$, $K_3\approx22$.
The static response test decides for A (Sec.~5): the response is close to isotropic, $K_1/K_3\approx0.8$--1, not 2. The splay/bend
difference in the mode decay rates (1.2--1.5 with the coupling on) is then a friction (backflow) effect, not a stiffness one.
\item \textbf{One correction applies in both readings.} MPCD multiplies the Jeffery rotation by $\chi_\mathrm{HI}=0.5$, so the alignment length is
$\ell_E=\sqrt{K/(\chi_\mathrm{HI}\gamma_1 s\sqrt{\lambda^2-1})}$. Our MPCD $\ell_E$ omitted $\chi_\mathrm{HI}$ (too short by $\sqrt2$). The ``$+16^\circ$''
boundary-layer estimate did include it.
\item \textbf{What is at stake.} Under A, every statement that rests on splay $\neq$ bend loses its mechanism: site pinning (80--110\,$\kT$), the static tilt
potential and its sign change with crowding, ``elasticity alone gives $35^\circ$'', the $+2$--$3^\circ$ residual, and the static elastic pull invoked
for the crowding drop. Energies computed with one constant ($K=32.5$) scale by 1.35 with unchanged ratios and signs. Under B the existing numbers
stand (anisotropy-dependent ones grow by about 12\%), but the anisotropy must be described as a property of the flow-coupled algorithm, not as bare
elasticity.
\item \textbf{A tension that exists already.} Passive seeded rings ($2k-2$ plus bulk pair; runs/rest\_pinning, coupling on) kept tilts of 60 and
$120^\circ$ for 100--300\,t.u.\ with no pull back to $0^\circ$. That is hard to square with an 80--110\,$\kT$ barrier at zero tilt even in reading B,
and the relaxed anisotropic energies of the seeding generator already disagreed with the first-order barrier (they fall with the offset).
\item \textbf{Site pinning, re-read (Sec.~\ref{sec:sites}).} What we measured as ``pinning'' (active wall defects at the same positions for every
initial condition) is explained without any anisotropy: the flow fixes the common tilt at $\arccos(1/\lambda)$, and the lock fixes each position
at the homeotropic site shifted by $\tau/(2k-2)$. The one-constant continuum does exactly this, and the passive MPCD data (registry frozen by the
initial condition, seeded tilts kept) are what it predicts without flow. The observation stands; the elastic barrier is not needed.
\item \textbf{What survives in both readings:} the lock $\tau_j=(2k-2)\phi_j+c$ and the $2|k-1|$ count; the leading tilt law $\arccos(1/\lambda)$; every
measured observable (counts, tilts, the crowding drop and its $d\sqrt s$ ordering, escapes, shapes, swirl tests); the signs and ratios of all ejection
and shielding energetics; $\la=22.2$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Site selection without anisotropy}\label{sec:sites}
\textbf{Two things were called pinning.} (1) The \emph{observation}: in active cavities the wall defects end at the same positions relative to the
anchoring for every initial condition (8 runs from 6 initial directors: arg $Z_{10}=\pm1.17$\,rad, lock-corrected offset $D=0.03\pm0.07$; only the
chirality is spontaneous), and with a modulated anchoring they follow the moved sites to 0.21--0.27 cells. (2) The \emph{mechanism} we attached to it:
splay $\neq$ bend makes each site an elastic minimum (first order in $\Delta$, barrier 80--110\,$\kT$ at $k=6$).

\textbf{The observation follows from the lock and the flow alone.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The lock (one-constant, exact): sliding a wall defect by $D$ rotates its far field, so the registry offset from the homeotropic sites equals
$\tau/(2k-2)$. The sites are fixed in space by the anchoring.
\item The flow: the no-slip shear at the wall turns the far field to the flow-aligned angle, so $\tau=\arccos(1/\lambda)\approx63^\circ$, with the sign
set by the circulation.
\item Together: once the flow fixes $\tau$, the lock fixes every position; all initial conditions end at the same registry up to the chirality.
With one Frank constant all registries cost the same at rest, and the flow is what picks one.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{The continuum shows the same site selection.} Measured with the MPCD estimator on the final fields of every continuum disk (one constant,
anchoring $\theta=k\phi$ as in MPCD): wherever the wall holds $2k-2$, the registry sits at the lock position, $D=0.01$--0.24\,rad (MPCD:
$0.03\pm0.07$), arg $Z\approx\pm1.1$\,rad for tilts of 51--$65^\circ$, with either chirality; $D$ grows slightly with crowding and forcing. So the
observation is not an MPCD peculiarity, and it needs no anisotropy: the continuum model has none. (Initial conditions were not varied in the
continuum; the final registry differs from the seeded $2k$ ring, so it was selected dynamically.)

\textbf{Orientation as a slow variable: the lock valley} (Fig.~\ref{fig:valley}; theory/lockvalley). With rigid cores of prescribed
orientation and the field relaxed outside, the layer energy depends on the registry shift and on the core orientation separately. With one constant
($K=44$) it is a valley: flat along the lock to within 1--1.5\,$\kT$ per defect (grid discretisation) and steep across it, with a twist stiffness of
24--28\,$\kT$/rad$^2$ per defect for sparse layers falling to 12 at $k=16$ (a $30^\circ$ twist off the lock costs 2--4\,$\kT$ per defect, $60^\circ$
6--16). So a $63^\circ$ tilt made by rotating cores in place would cost $\approx10$--20\,$\kT$ per defect, while the same tilt made by sliding along
the valley costs nothing: elasticity enforces the lock, the flow chooses the point along it. With the effective anisotropy (43.1/21.9) the valley
acquires a floor at $\tau_\mathrm{lock}=0$ (6--8\,$\kT$ per defect at $180^\circ$; 12 at $k=4$), the first-order barrier of before, and the twist walls
are about one third softer. The twist stiffness softens with crowding in both cases, so crowded layers hold the lock less firmly.

\textbf{What the lock + flow picture still has to supply} is the stiffness of the selected registry: the measured collective rms is 0.29--0.36
cells. It would come from the flow-alignment restoring torque through the boundary layer, which the flat-wall model can estimate.

\textbf{Direct test: passive $\to$ active switch} (runs/switchon; Fig.~\ref{fig:switchon}). The 8 passive runs of runs/pinning\_ic (12 wall
defects, registries frozen by 8 different initial directors: arg $Z_{12}$ from $-2.62$ to $+1.20$) were resumed with activity 0.07. All 8 end at
the registry of the runs that were active from the start: arg $Z_{10}=\pm1.16$--1.18 ($|Z|=1.00$), lock-corrected $D=\pm0.06$--0.07, tilt
63--$65^\circ$, circulation 0.86--0.96 (5 of one sign, 3 of the other). The passive registry is not slid into place: activity first melts the layer
(12 $\to$ 2--4 wall defects within $\approx50$\,t.u.), the coherent flow then builds over 200--800\,t.u., the tilt settles at $\approx63^\circ$ once
the circulation passes $\approx0.6$, and the wall refills to 10 at the flow-selected registry, erasing the initial one.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{../master/theory/switchon/fig_switchon.pdf}
\caption{Passive $\to$ active switch, 8 initial conditions: wall count, circulation, tilt and registry against time after the switch.}\label{fig:switchon}\end{figure}

\textbf{Consequence for the text.} Keep the observation, call it site selection by the flow through the lock, and drop the elastic barrier as its
cause (rows 11--17 below). This holds in reading A, and in reading B the anisotropy is at most a correction to it.

\section{What would need redoing}
\textbf{If A (bare isotropic response):}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Site selection (row 15) by the flow through the lock (Sec.~\ref{sec:sites}); what remains is the registry stiffness, from the flow-alignment

\section{Static response test}
Passive channel, planar or homeotropic walls, a field tilted $10^\circ$ off the wall axis; the healing length $\xi$ of the time-averaged profile gives
$K_1/K_3=(\xi_\mathrm{splay}/\xi_\mathrm{bend})^2$ (reports/master/theory/kresponse). Fits with the bulk angle fixed at the field angle (the free-bulk
fits are degenerate when $\xi$ is a sizeable fraction of the half-width). Errors from time blocks (and seeds for the wide channel):

\textbf{Result.} The response is close to isotropic at every scale tested (3--18 cells): with the coupling on, $K_1/K_3=0.74$--0.94 (weighted mean
$\approx0.81\pm0.07$), against $\approx0.96\pm0.05$ with it off. The factor 2 of the fluctuations is absent from the static response
($>10\sigma$). This is reading A: the defects respond to an essentially isotropic stiffness ($\approx44\,\kT$); the flow coupling makes the
\emph{noise} anisotropic. A small response difference between coupling on and off ($\approx0.8$ vs 1, of the opposite sign to the fluctuations)
is at the $2\sigma$ level and may be a fit systematic.
